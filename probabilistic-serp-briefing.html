One SERP, Many Draws
A guided briefing for the SEO team on Mike King's "probabilistic SERP" article and how our process compares.
    Start here

    Google is starting to build a different results page for every search.

    Part 1 · What's changing

    How Google is starting to write results pages with AI, and why no two people see quite the same page anymore.

    Part 2 · The data problem

    Google is feeding fake-but-normal-looking pages to rank trackers. What that does to reports.

    Why our topic-level, Search Console-first approach already avoids most of this, and where we still need to be careful.

    Where this comes from

    Mike King runs the agency iPullRank and is one of the most respected technical SEOs in the industry. In October 2026 he published a long article arguing that the Google results page has become "probabilistic": instead of one fixed list, it's closer to a roll of the dice each time someone searches.

    In plain terms

    If the page changes every time it's loaded, then a single screenshot or a single daily rank check can't tell you how a client is really doing. You'd need many checks and look at the overall pattern.

    Why it matters to us

    Clients and account managers often react to rank reports. If those reports are getting less reliable, we need to know why, what to trust instead, and how to explain it. Good news: our process is already built around the more reliable data.

    Start here

    Eight terms you'll see throughout.

    SERP
    "Search engine results page." The page Google shows after you search: ads, the map pack, AI Overview, blue links, People Also Ask, and so on.
    Rank tracker
    A tool that searches Google automatically, saves the results page and records where a site shows up. Think of it as a robot that takes a screenshot each day.
    DataForSEO
    A service that runs Google searches for you and returns the results as data. Our AI visibility and local pack tracking use it. SerpApi is a similar service that Google sued in December 2025.
    Google Search Console (GSC)
    Google's own report of how real people found a site: impressions (times shown), clicks and the queries behind them. It comes from actual searchers, not a robot.
    AI Overview / AI Mode
    The AI-written summary at the top of results (Overview), and Google's chat-style search experience (AI Mode).
    Generative UI
    When an AI doesn't just write text, it designs and builds the page itself, including calculators, comparison tables and planners, fresh for each search.
    Retrieval vs. presentation
    Retrieval is which pages Google picks as sources. Presentation is how it shows them. This article says the second is changing, not the first.
    Poisoned SERP
    A results page that looks completely normal but has most of the usual websites quietly swapped for unrelated ones. Seen mostly by suspected robots, like rank trackers.

    For 20 years, the results page was a stack of fixed blocks.

    What you're looking at

    A simplified Google results page. Google built each block (ads, map pack, blue links, People Also Ask) ahead of time. For each search, it just decided which blocks to show, in what order, and filled them in.

    In plain terms

    Because the page was built from the same parts every time, it was roughly the same for everyone in the same place. So a robot could load it once a day, count down the list, and say "your client is #2." That number meant something.

    Why it matters to us

    All of traditional SEO reporting, including rank tracking, "position" talk with clients and click-through estimates, assumes this kind of stable list exists. The rest of this briefing is about that assumption breaking down.

    Now an AI can write the whole page from scratch, for each search.

    Single tooth · with crown$3,900–$5,200
    Illustration. Figures are placeholders, not real pricing.
    What you're looking at

    How Google's research system works, based on a Google paper called "Generative UI: LLMs are Effective UI Generators." A search goes in. The AI gets three things: tools to search Google and get images, instructions that tell it what to build, and a clean-up step that fixes errors before the page reaches you.

    In plain terms

    Instead of filling in pre-built blocks, the AI reads your question, decides what you'd find useful, searches for the facts, and then writes a custom page, often with something you can interact with. The instructions even say a question like "what time is it in Tel Aviv" should come back as a working clock, not a sentence. Every fact on the page must come from a search result, and anything without real data behind it gets removed.

    Why it matters to us

    A question like "how much does a dental implant cost" could become a cost calculator built from our clients' pricing pages. The patient gets an answer without ever visiting the practice's site. Our pages become ingredients for Google's page instead of the destination.

    Part 1 · What's changing

    There are three levels of "AI-built page." Google's results sit in the middle, heading right.

    Static

    The AI picks from a fixed set of hand-built blocks. Developers control the look. The AI only decides which blocks appear.

    Today's Google results

    Declarative

    The AI writes a description ("a table here, a chart there, three cards") and the app builds it from a library of parts.

    Where Google is adding pieces now

    Open-ended

    The AI writes the entire page's code itself. Total freedom, but more risk of broken or odd pages.

    Google's research paper
    ← More control, more predictableMore flexible, less predictable →
    What you're looking at

    Google Cloud describes AI-built interfaces as a spectrum. King maps today's results page onto it: it's mostly static (fixed blocks), with Google now adding declarative pieces like interactive visuals, tables, graphs and trackers that it arranges into custom layouts. The research paper is the far-right open-ended version.

    Why it matters to us

    This explains why the poisoned pages later in this briefing still looked normal: the template stays the same while the content inside it gets swapped. The further right Google moves, the less any two results pages will resemble each other.

    In plain terms

    Google has every reason to keep going: users like it, it keeps them on Google longer, and the technical problems are going away. We should plan as if this becomes normal, not treat it as an experiment.

    What King found

    He built a copy of Google's system and ran ten everyday searches. Every page came back different, with its own interactive pieces:

    • "How much does a kitchen remodel cost" → a cost estimator with a live total
    • "Roth IRA vs 401k" → sliders comparing after-tax value, plus a side-by-side table
    • "Should I buy or rent" → a break-even simulator with a verdict
    • "NYC to Boston train vs bus" → comparison cards with filters
    • "Things to do in Lisbon" → a 3-day trip planner with a checklist

    One of the ten pages (half-marathon training) was partly broken by a code error.

    Why it matters to us

    Each of these pieces does the job a website used to do. For dental, think cost pages, "X vs Y" pages and insurance pages. If the facts on those pages are clear and specific, we're more likely to be the source the AI uses and cites. If they're vague, the AI pulls from someone else.

    Part 1 · What's changing

    Google still picks sources the same way. What changes is how it shows them.

    ↑ presentation: different every time ↑
    Retrieval: practice-a.comyour clientada.orgpractice-b.comsame pool of sources
    Three people search the same thing. Google pulls from the same sources but builds three different pages.
    What you're looking at

    The bottom layer is retrieval: the set of pages Google picks as relevant. That's the same system that already powers AI Overviews. The top layer is presentation: the page each person actually sees. That's what generative UI changes.

    In plain terms

    King's main point: the way to earn visibility hasn't changed. Be relevant, be clear, be the best source. What's broken is how we measure it, because there's no single page left to measure. Each page is one draw from a range of possible pages.

    Why it matters to us

    Our content work doesn't need to change direction. Our reporting needs to stop treating any single snapshot as the truth.

    Rank trackers are getting pages that look normal and are quietly wrong.

    All nine vendors on page one were gone three days later. The page size barely changed (720 KB → 728 KB), so nothing looked broken.
    What you're looking at

    A real example from the rank-tracking company Nozzle. On October 4, a search for "white label local seo" showed nine relevant vendors. On October 7, the same search returned a steakhouse, a WordPress tool, Reddit and government sites. Google read "local" literally and served nonsense.

    In plain terms

    That's a poisoned SERP. There's no error message and no "are you a robot?" check. The page looks complete. The only way Nozzle spotted it was by comparing each keyword to its own recent history.

    Why Google would do this

    Nozzle believes Google is fighting bulk scraping, where companies copy its results to feed AI products. Rank trackers look like scrapers, so they get caught too. King adds that AI-built pages make this cheap: it's easy to hand a suspected robot a believable but wrong page. Real searchers probably never see these.

    Why it matters to us

    Any tool that scrapes Google, including DataForSEO, can receive poisoned pages without knowing it. A client could look like they dropped off page one when nothing actually happened.

    Part 2 · The data problem

    It went from rare to one in four in a few weeks.

    In plain terms

    In August, fewer than 2 in 1,000 results were poisoned. By mid-September it was 8 in 100. For three days at the end of September, roughly a quarter of every results page these trackers collected was fake.

    Some industries got hit harder
    • Legal searches peaked at 51.3% poisoned on Sept 27
    • "Near me" searches: 11.4%, vs 7.8% for everything else
    • Brand-name searches were lowest at 5.3%
    Why it matters to us

    Our clients live on local, "near me"-style searches, which are hit harder than average. If we relied on rank trackers, dental would be one of the noisiest categories to report on. Brand searches ("Smith Family Dental") are the safest.

    Part 2 · The data problem

    It comes and goes fast, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

    80%of poisoning lasted for just one check, then the page went back to normal
    77.9%of poisoned keywords had their usual sites back within three days
    58.6%of the time, a site that usually ranks top 10 vanished from a poisoned page (vs 2.7% normally)
    1–7% vs 9–30%poisoning rate seen by two different collection methods on the same days
    In plain terms

    Picture this: a client's report shows them falling off page one for "dentist near me." The account manager panics and calls the client. Three days later, everything is back. There was no real drop. The tracker just got a poisoned page that day.

    Worse, two tools checking the same keywords on the same days can disagree wildly depending on how they collect data, so you can't fix it by switching vendors.

    Why it matters to us

    Never react to a single bad scraped result. A real ranking loss persists and shows up in Search Console clicks. A poisoned page doesn't. This is the single most practical lesson in the article.

    Part 2 · His tool

    King built a copy of Google's system to test all this. It's a research demo, not a product.

    What it is

    Genraggle is a homemade simulator. King took the instructions from Google's paper, gave Gemini a Google search tool, and let it build results pages so he could watch how it behaves. He added one thing Google's paper doesn't have: a request to cite every fact, so he could see which websites get used.

    What it's for

    The poison switch is the useful part. Turn it on and some search results get quietly replaced. If a client's citation disappears as soon as one result changes, it's fragile. If it survives, the content is doing real work. It's a stress test.

    Our take

    Useful for building intuition and showing clients where search is going. Not useful as measurement: it has no real Google index, no personalization and no click data. King says so himself. It's similar in spirit to our own passage-level retrieval simulator.

    Part 2 · His fix

    His answer: treat every check as one sample, and take lots of them.

    1 · Check several times a day

    Not needed

    Compare each new capture with the last good one. If most of the usual sites are gone, flag it as poisoned and throw it out.

    We don't track keywords, so we don't have this problem to solve.

    2 · Report a rate, not a rank

    Agree

    "Cited in 13 of 18 checks, give or take" instead of "position 4 on Tuesday."

    Fits our AI visibility reporting. Worth using there.

    3 · Treat average position as a trend only

    Already do

    Search Console's "average position 7.3" is an average of many pages. Nobody ever saw position 7.3.

    We already read it as direction, not a number.

    4 · Sample AI answers repeatedly

    Agree

    Run the same question many times and count how often the brand gets mentioned.

    This is how AI visibility tracking already works.

    5 · Split by location and persona

    Agree

    Results differ by place and person. The differences are information, not noise.

    Especially true for local practices.

    6 · Anchor everything to Search Console

    Built in

    If clicks hold steady while a tracker crashes, doubt the tracker.

    This is the core of our new tool.

    7 · Stress-test with a simulator

    Optional

    Use something like Genraggle to see how fragile a citation is.

    Good for learning, not for client reporting.

    His warning

    Cost

    Rank tracking will get much more expensive, because you need many more checks to get one trustworthy number.

    Another reason not to build on keyword tracking.

    We don't track keywords at all. We track topics, pages and the whole site over time.

    What you're looking at

    Where our numbers come from. Green sources record what real people did. They can't be poisoned, because Google isn't trying to fool its own reports. DataForSEO is the only scraped source, and we use it as a supporting signal, never as the final word.

    Why topics instead of keywords

    One keyword is one noisy check. A topic adds up dozens of related searches, so random swings and the odd poisoned page average out. It also matches how patients actually search: in lots of different ways, across Google, maps, AI answers, reviews and social.

    Why it matters

    Most of King's fixes exist to rescue keyword rank tracking. We already skipped that step. Our job is to make sure the scraped data we do use is always checked against the real data.

    How to use the grid

    Every time the scraped data moves, look at what Search Console did. Only the top-left box, where both drop, is a real loss worth raising with the client. The top-right box is the poisoning case: wait and recheck. The bottom-left box means rankings held but fewer people searched or clicked, which is a different problem.

    Why it matters

    This is King's "anchor to Search Console" advice, but built into the tool itself instead of left to someone remembering to check. It stops false alarms before they reach a client.

    What you're looking at

    Two lines falling at the same angle. The grey line is how much people nationally searched for a topic (from Google Trends). The blue line is how many clicks the client got for that topic (from Search Console).

    A real example

    One of our clients had a main topic that depended mostly on a single article. Its traffic fell year over year. When we checked Google Trends, interest in that topic had fallen nationally by the same amount. The client wasn't losing to competitors. They were riding a wave that was going down for everyone.

    The simple math

    Divide the client's clicks by the topic's demand. If that number stays flat while both fall, the client held their share. If the client falls faster than demand, they lost ground and we should act.

    Why it matters

    "Your traffic is down" and "the whole topic is down and you kept your share" are very different conversations. King's article doesn't cover this. It's one of the strongest parts of our approach.

    Where we agree with him

    The way to earn visibility hasn't changed: be relevant, clear and specific. AI-built pages will reward content with plain, citable facts. And a single snapshot of a results page should never be treated as the truth.

    Where we go further

    Part 3 · Our answer

    Our approach has weak spots too. Here's how we stay honest.

    Keep re-checking the match

    Our trust comes from the tool matching real Search Console data. Conditions change fast (poisoning went from almost nothing to 25% in weeks).

    Do: regularly compare the tool's output against connected clients. If the match starts slipping, prospect estimates are slipping too.

    Remember our clients are not average

    Our clients get our SEO, so they probably do better than a typical practice in their specialty.

    Say: "Here's how pediatric practices we work with are trending." Not: "Here's the whole market."

    Watch small groups

    If we only have three orthodontists in a region, one unusual practice can skew the whole trend.

    Do: note the group size, and widen to state or national cohorts when the local one is thin.

    Seen but not clicked

    When Google builds a calculator from a client's page, patients may get their answer without visiting.

    Do: if impressions hold but clicks fall, consider a format change before calling it a loss. Use AI visibility sampling to fill the gap.

    Search Console has blind spots

    It runs a couple of days behind, and small practices often have queries hidden for privacy.

    Do: lean on topic totals and GBP data where individual queries are hidden.

    Make pages easy to cite

    AI-built calculators and comparisons need specific facts to pull from.

    Do: put clear prices or ranges, coverage details and plain comparisons on implant, Invisalign, insurance and cost pages.

    Wrap-up

    What this means for your day-to-day.

    If you talk to clients

    • Lead with Search Console clicks, calls and GBP actions.
    • If a client sends a scary ranking screenshot, check Search Console before reacting. Most single-day drops aren't real.
    • Explain shrinking topics as market shifts when Trends backs it up.

    If you write content

    • State facts plainly: prices or ranges, timelines, coverage, who it's for.
    • Build clear "X vs Y" and cost pages. Those are what AI turns into comparison cards and calculators.
    • Same fundamentals as before: be the most useful, specific source.

    If you work on data or the tool

    • Treat DataForSEO as a signal to check, never as the answer.
    • Flag any scraped drop that Search Console doesn't confirm.
    • Compare topic performance against Google Trends before calling it a loss.
    • Keep validating the tool against connected clients.

    If you prep prospect audits

    • Start from the specialty cohort: which topics are growing for practices like this one.
    • Compare against what the prospect's site covers.
    • Frame findings as our clients' results, not the whole market.

    The one-line version: the way to earn visibility hasn't changed, but a single snapshot of Google can no longer be trusted. We trust real searcher data first and check everything else against it.
