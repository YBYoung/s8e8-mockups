Medicaid, not Medicare. Medicare is for people 65 and older, or people who have been on Social Security Disability for two years. What she can get now is Ohio Medicaid, which pays for doctors, medications, equipment, and in-home nursing and aides. Social Security Disability (step 4) is the road that eventually leads to Medicare.

One rule that makes everything faster: keep one folder (paper or on your phone) with every form, every confirmation number, and the name of every person you talk to. Write the date on everything. The log at the bottom of this page is for that.

2

Make yourself her official representative

Same day as step 3, or the day before.

Because she can't fill out forms or talk to caseworkers, Ohio needs a signed form saying you can act for her. Without it, the county can't legally discuss her case with you.

If she truly cannot make any mark at all, tell the county when you apply. Federal Medicaid rules let someone acting responsibly for an incapacitated adult apply for them, and the county has a process for it that usually involves a doctor's statement. That's one more reason a doctor who has seen her (step 6) matters.

Next: step 3, apply for Medicaid

3

Apply for Ohio Medicaid

This week. The county has 45 days to decide, or 90 days if you tell them she is disabled. Most cases finish sooner.

Apply as an adult under 65 with no income. That's the fastest track, and she qualifies on income alone. You'll still describe her disability in the application; it matters later.

Pick one way to apply

What to say and ask for

When you start

I'm applying for Medicaid on behalf of my adult daughter as her authorized representative. She is bedbound with a serious illness, has no income, and cannot communicate right now. I have her Social Security number and documents.

Next: step 4, Social Security (start it the same week)

4

Apply for Social Security disability

Start the same week as Medicaid. First decisions take about 6 months on average.

Two programs, one application. SSDI is for people who worked and paid in; it pays more and leads to Medicare two years after payments begin. SSI is for people with little or no work history; it pays up to $994 a month (2026) and keeps her Medicaid going automatically. Social Security checks her record and puts her in the right one, so you don't have to decide.

Start the claim

1-800-772-1213Social Security. Mon to Fri 8am to 7pm.

Give the claim its best chance

ME/CFS claims are winnable but often denied the first time, because Social Security relies on medical records and she may have few. Two things fix that: a doctor who has actually examined her (step 6), and a written statement from that doctor describing her limits. There's a box in step 6 with exactly what that doctor needs to write down. Don't wait to apply, but get a doctor involved fast.

Next: step 5, the waiver (the day her Medicaid is approved)

5

Request the Ohio Home Care Waiver

The day her Medicaid is approved. Assessment within about 30 days, enrollment targeted within 45.

Medicaid pays her doctors and a limited amount of home nursing (see step 6). The Ohio Home Care Waiver is what pays for aide hours, nursing hours, supplies, and a case manager in the home, for Ohioans under 60 with a physical disability. She has to be on Medicaid first, which is why this is step 5. There is no waiting list.

1-844-644-6582Ohio Benefits Long-Term Services and Supports. Mon to Fri 9am to 5pm.

Timing: Ohio is replacing this intake system in early October 2026. The state's site says requests go in before 4pm Thursday, October 1, and the new system opens Monday, October 5. If you call in between and get nowhere, wait until October 5 and call the same number again, or use the paper form below.

You can be paid to care for her. Because you're her authorized representative, Ohio's rules let a parent of an adult be paid as her personal care aide, up to 40 hours a week. Two ways: get hired by a home care agency that serves her, or through "self-direction," where she is the employer and a state payroll company (PPL) pays you. Ask the case manager for self-directed services. One catch: if you're the paid caregiver, someone else has to be her self-direction "representative," or she has to sign as employer herself. There's also a daily-rate program called Structured Family Caregiving; ask, but it can't replace nursing for someone medically complex.

Heads up: Ohio has paused enrolling new home health agencies and independent aides into Medicaid through November 14, 2026, and it may be extended. Existing agencies are fine, but you may need to call several before one has an opening. The pause does not stop you from being hired as a self-directed caregiver. The case manager and her managed care plan can help you find an agency.

For the doctor: two things this patient needs from you

1. Do not prescribe exercise or a "build up gradually" program. The defining feature of ME/CFS is post-exertional malaise: exertion beyond the patient's limit causes a delayed crash lasting days or weeks, and in severe patients can be permanent. The CDC states: "Standard exercise recommendations for healthy people can substantially harm patients with ME/CFS," and that the harm is prevented by activity management (pacing) within the patient's "energy envelope." The UK's NICE guideline (NG206, 2021) says: "Do not offer people with ME/CFS ... any programme that ... uses fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise, for example, graded exercise therapy." For completely bedbound patients the CDC advises in-home visits, phone or video check-ups, and a modified approach to activity, along with screening for pressure sores, deconditioning, and nutrition. Sound, light and touch sensitivity are part of the illness; dim the room and keep visits short.

2. Document the illness the way Social Security needs it. Under Social Security Ruling 14-1p, ME/CFS counts as a medically determinable impairment when a physician records clinical signs or lab findings over at least six consecutive months, not symptoms alone. Signs the ruling lists: palpably swollen or tender lymph nodes, non-exudative pharyngitis, persistent reproducible muscle tenderness on repeated exams, and any other signs consistent with accepted clinical practice. Findings it lists: neurally mediated hypotension on tilt-table or another accepted test, abnormal brain MRI, abnormal sleep study or exercise test, and documented cognitive deficits on mental status exam. Please record whichever of these are present, describe her functional limits in concrete terms (sitting, standing, speaking, tolerating light and sound, toileting, eating), and note how long she has been this way.

Sources: CDC, "Strategies to Prevent Worsening of Symptoms" and "ME/CFS Clinical Care for Severely Affected Patients" (cdc.gov/me-cfs); NICE NG206 (2021); National Academies, "Beyond ME/CFS: Redefining an Illness" (2015); SSA, SSR 14-1p.
